# Writing a video people click and keep watching

This is the studio's guide for the story of a video: what it promises, how it opens, how it
holds attention and how it ends. The design guide says how a frame looks. This guide says
why someone clicks and why they stay. Follow it for every outline and every scene list.
When the person's own request or a document marked "model" says something different, that
wins.

A video is recommended when people who are shown it click, keep watching and are glad they
did. Each rule below serves one of those three. None of them is a trick: a video that
promises what it does not deliver loses the viewer and the recommendation with them.

## The promise

Decide the promise before you plan anything else. It is one sentence: what this viewer will
know, see or be able to do by the end that they cannot now, and why they would care.

- Name the viewer. "Someone who uses GPS every day and has never wondered how it works" is
  a viewer. "Everyone" is not.
- Name the gap: the question the viewer cannot answer yet, or the thing they believe that
  is not true. A good gap is specific and a little surprising. "How GPS works" is a topic.
  "Why your phone needs a fourth satellite to tell the time" is a gap.
- Write the promise as a working title of at most 60 characters and as a thumbnail idea of
  one sentence. If you cannot write both, the video has no promise yet. The title, the
  thumbnail and the first sentence of the video all make the same promise, each in its own
  words.
- Everything in the video either pays the promise off or is cut. A fact that is true and
  interesting but off the promise belongs in another video.

## Start from what the viewer believes

When the subject is one people think they already understand, do not open with the correct
explanation. A viewer who hears something that sounds like what they already believe stops
listening and leaves surer of the wrong idea.

- State the common belief first, in the viewer's own words, and let it stand for a moment.
- Then show where it breaks: the case it cannot explain.
- Only then give the explanation that works.

Use this where there is a real, common misconception. Do not invent one.

## The first thirty seconds

Most viewers who leave, leave here. YouTube measures an opening by how many are still
watching after thirty seconds, which is about 75 words of narration.

- The first sentence delivers on the title. It is the question, the surprising fact or the
  wrong belief itself, said plainly. Not a greeting, not the channel, not "in this video",
  not background the viewer did not ask for.
- The first picture is the most striking true picture the video has, and something in it
  moves within the first second. Never open on a title card or on empty space filling up.
- By fifteen seconds the viewer knows what they will get and why it matters to them: the
  stakes. One sentence.
- By thirty seconds the first real piece of the answer has arrived. A viewer who clicked
  for an answer and is still being introduced to the topic leaves.
- Hold back the full answer, not the start of it. Give enough to prove the video can
  deliver, and leave the question open.

## Order by curiosity, not by textbook

A textbook starts with definitions and builds up. A video starts with the question and
earns each definition when the viewer needs it.

- Each chapter answers one question, and its answer raises the next one. Write the outline
  as that chain of questions. If a chapter raises no question, it is the last chapter or it
  is in the wrong place.
- Put the strongest material early. Audiences shrink through a video, so the best moment
  placed last is seen by the fewest people.
- Introduce a term at the moment it is needed to answer the open question, never before.
- A chapter opens by saying or showing what is at stake in it, in one sentence, so that a
  viewer who drifted has a reason to return. A chapter exported by itself must make sense
  from its first sentence.

## But and therefore

Read the narration of a chapter as a chain of beats. Between each two beats, one of three
words fits: "and", "but" or "therefore".

- "But" and "therefore" make a story: something is true, but there is a problem, therefore
  something follows.
- "And" makes a list. Two "and" joins in a row is a place where the viewer's attention
  drifts. Reorder, cut, or find the "but".
- The words themselves need not be spoken. "So", "which means", "except" and a plain new
  sentence all do the work. The relation between the beats is what matters.
- Each chapter has at least one "but": a turn where what the viewer expected is not what
  happens.

## Holding the middle

- Every scene tells the viewer something new or moves the question forward. A scene that
  restates, or that only decorates, is cut.
- Open a question before you answer it. "So how does the receiver know the time?" then the
  answer, not the answer alone.
- Signpost what is coming, and leave a little of it unsaid: "there is one more problem, and
  it is the strange one".
- Change something the viewer can see or hear at least every twenty to thirty seconds: a
  new kind of picture, a change of scale, a number, an example after an abstraction.
  Change that carries no meaning does not count.
- After an abstract idea comes a concrete one: an example, a number, a thing you can
  picture.
- Do not announce, apologise or stall: no "before we begin", "as mentioned", "let's dive
  in", "you might be wondering".

## The ending

- End on the payoff: the answer to the question the video opened with, in one or two
  sentences, in its strongest form. The last line should be the one a viewer would repeat
  to someone else.
- Then stop. No list of what was covered, no "thanks for watching", no call to subscribe.
  A viewer who hears the video wrapping up leaves, and how much of the video they watched
  is what the video is judged on.
- One new thought as the last line is welcome when it is true: what this changes, or the
  next question it opens.

## A Short is a different thing

A Short is swiped to, not chosen. There is no title or thumbnail to make the promise, so
the first second has to.

- The first second shows the result or says the surprising thing. No lead-in at all.
- One idea, one payoff, and the payoff arrives before the viewer expects it to.
- It must make sense to someone who has never seen the long video.
- The first sentence of the chapter most worth exporting as a Short should work as the
  first sentence of a Short.

## Before you hand the plan or the script over

Answer each of these. If one answer is no, fix it.

1. Can you say the promise in one sentence, and does the title make it?
2. Does the first sentence of the video deliver on the title, with nothing before it?
3. Has the first real piece of the answer arrived by thirty seconds?
4. Does every chapter answer a question and, except the last, raise the next?
5. Is there a "but" in every chapter, and no run of "and then"?
6. Does every scene say something no other scene says?
7. Is the strongest material in the first half?
8. Does the video end on its answer and then stop?
9. Is every promise the title and the opening make kept?
