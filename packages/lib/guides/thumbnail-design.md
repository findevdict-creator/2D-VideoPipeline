# Designing a thumbnail

A thumbnail has one job: make the right viewer stop scrolling and click. Most people see it
at the size of a postage stamp, next to nine others, for less than a second. This guide is
how the studio designs one. A thumbnail is not a frame of the video and not a title card.

## Start from the click, not from the video

1. Write down who the viewer is and what they are curious about before they have seen
   anything.
2. Find the single most surprising, valuable or strange thing the video shows or proves.
   That is the promise. The thumbnail shows the promise, and only the promise.
3. The thumbnail and the title work as a pair. The title states the topic; the thumbnail
   adds the feeling, the stakes or the question. Never repeat the title's words in the
   thumbnail.
4. The promise must be true. A thumbnail that the video does not pay off costs watch time,
   and watch time is what the click was for.

## The three-element rule

A thumbnail has at most three elements:

- **one subject**: the object, diagram or character the video is about, drawn large;
- **one short text**: zero to four words;
- **one emphasis device**, if it helps: an arrow, a circle, a "vs", a big number.

Everything else is removed. If you cannot delete an element without losing the idea, it
stays; otherwise it goes.

## Curiosity

Give the viewer a question they can only answer by clicking. Ways that work for explainer
videos:

- **A gap**: show the result without the cause, or the before without the after ("Why does
  this float?").
- **A contrast**: two things side by side that should not be equal, or one thing that is
  far bigger than expected. Before and after, expected and real, small and huge.
- **A number with stakes**: "$1,000,000", "0.5", "165 years", set huge. A number is more
  specific than an adjective and reads at any size.
- **Something that looks wrong**: an everyday object doing what it should not.
- **A pointer at a small detail**: an arrow or circle on one spot says "look here, this is
  the secret" without giving it away.

Do not answer the question in the thumbnail.

## Text

- Zero to four words. Three is the usual best. Under 20 characters in total.
- Words carry emotion or stakes, not description: "UNSOLVED", "$1M PRIZE", "IT'S WRONG",
  not "The Riemann Hypothesis Explained".
- Set it huge: the capital letters at least 15 percent of the frame's height, which is 110
  pixels or more on a 720-pixel-high frame. Two lines at most, one to two words a line.
- The heaviest weight the font has (800 to 900). All capitals is fine for one to three
  words.
- Give every letter a dark outline of 8 to 12 pixels or a solid block behind the words, so
  the text holds on any background. Fill in white, or in the one bright colour.
- One word may take the bright colour, the one that carries the stakes.
- Keep text out of the bottom-right corner: the video's length is printed there. Keep it
  inside a margin of 5 percent of the frame on every side.
- Put the text on the left or the top and the subject on the right, or the other way round.
  Never text across the subject.

## The subject

- One subject, filling 40 to 60 percent of the frame. Crop it boldly: it may run off an
  edge.
- Draw it simpler than in the video: thicker outlines (8 pixels or more), fewer parts,
  larger features. Detail under 12 pixels wide does not exist at stamp size.
- Separate it from the background with a rim: a light outline, a glow or a clear change of
  brightness. The subject's outline should be readable as a silhouette.
- If the video has a character, use it, large, with a strong expression (surprised,
  puzzled, shocked) and looking or pointing at the other element. Faces and eyes pull
  attention more than anything else. If it has no character, do not invent a face.
- An explainer's subject is often a diagram. Then show the one moment of the diagram where
  something is visibly at stake, not the tidy finished version.

## Colour and contrast

- Bright and saturated beats subtle. Thumbnails sit on white, on dark grey and on black,
  so neither a white nor a black background separates on its own: use a saturated or a
  strongly graded background, or a dark one with a bright subject and rim.
- Two or three colours in total, far apart: one for the background, one for the subject,
  one bright one for the emphasis. Yellow, red, orange, cyan and bright green read best
  small. Start from the video's theme so the thumbnail belongs to the video, and push its
  colours brighter if they are muted.
- The contrast between the text and what is behind it should be extreme, the kind you can
  read with your eyes half shut.
- Add depth cheaply: a radial gradient that is lighter behind the subject and darker at
  the corners pulls the eye in.
- Avoid large areas of pure red text on pure blue, or thin light text on mid-tones.

## Composition

- The frame is 1280 by 720. Compose on thirds: the subject's centre on one vertical third
  line, the text block on the other.
- Make a clear reading order: the eye lands on the subject, moves to the text, and ends on
  the emphasis device, or text first and then the subject. Lines, arrows and a character's
  gaze should lead along that path, not out of the frame.
- Leave some empty background. A crowded thumbnail reads as noise.
- Tilt is allowed: text or the subject rotated 3 to 6 degrees adds energy. Rotate one
  thing, not everything.
- Arrows are thick (16 pixels or more), curved, in the bright colour, with a dark outline,
  and point at one precise spot.

## The stamp test

You are shown the thumbnail twice: full size and at 320 by 180. Judge the small one.

1. In one second, can you say what the video is about and why you would click?
2. Can you read every word?
3. Is there one obvious thing to look at first?
4. Does the subject separate from the background?
5. Is anything in it that you would not miss if it were gone?
6. Does it avoid repeating the title?
7. Is the bottom-right corner free of anything important?
8. Would it stand out in a row of other thumbnails on the same topic, which are probably
   dark blue with white text?

If any answer is no, change the design and look again. Two or three rounds are normal.

## What not to do

- No tiny text, no sentences, no bullet points, no logos, no channel name.
- No screenshot of the video with a caption on it.
- No more than three colours, no rainbow gradients, no thin lines.
- No promise the video does not keep, no fake "play" button, no red circle around nothing.
- No motion: it is one still picture. Do not use entrances, cues or anything that depends
  on the frame number.
